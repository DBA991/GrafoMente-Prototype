# The Graph as Mind

*Prototypes, symbols, and progressive fetch: a hypothesis for a cognitive architecture in artificial intelligence*

*A philosophical and technical exploration spanning Platonic Ideas, Rosch's Prototype Theory, and the frontiers of neuro-symbolic AI — written by an outside observer*

---

## A necessary preface

This article begins with a question from a non-expert, posed at the margins of a dense and rapidly evolving technical field. It is not an engineering proposal: it is a speculative hypothesis, constructed by analogy between philosophical traditions and existing research directions. The ideas presented here have not been verified by anyone with technical expertise in the field, and the reader is invited to treat them accordingly — as intuitions that may or may not make sense in their implementational details, offered here for the value of the question they open, not for the solidity of the answer they propose.

The context is a [previous article](https://medium.com/) in which the author examined how modern artificial intelligence systems process language — through tokens, vectors, and statistical co-occurrences — and what would be missing from a system that wished to operate on *concepts* rather than *words*. In that context, a mechanism called **progressive fetch** and a graph structure were named as possible alternatives to retention-based training, without elaboration. This article attempts to open that direction, following the thread back to its theoretical roots and forward to the edges of what current research has already explored.

## Plato's Ideas as structural intuition

Everything begins with a very ancient intuition.

In the *Phaedo* and the *Republic*, [Plato](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedo.html) proposes that perceptible reality is the shadow of a system of pure Forms — immutable Ideas inhabiting the Hyperuranion. **Horseness** exists independently of any specific horse: every horse is an imperfect participation in that Form. The same structure applies to circularity, justice, beauty. The things of the world are instances; the Ideas are the underlying structure that makes them recognizable.

It is obviously impossible to transfer Platonic metaphysics directly into a computational architecture. That is not the objective. What is worth preserving, however, is the structural intuition: there exists a level of representation that is *abstract with respect to instances*, stable with respect to perceptual variation, and accessible not through enumeration but through something Plato would call *participation* — the recognition of a common form across different examples.

A child who has seen ten different horses does not memorize ten images. They construct something — a concept, a prototype, an internal structure — and use it to recognize the eleventh horse they have never seen. What this internal structure is, and how it works, is precisely the question to which twentieth-century cognitive psychology gave its most precise answer.

## Eleanor Rosch and the structure of prototypes

In the 1970s, psychologist **Eleanor Rosch** conducted a series of experiments that changed the way linguistics and psychology think about categories. The results, published in a sequence of foundational papers — including [*Natural Categories* (1973)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90017-0) and [*Principles of Categorization* (1978)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/B9780122736308500136) — demolished the classical Aristotelian model, according to which categories are sets of necessary and sufficient conditions.

Rosch's thesis is that cognitive categories do not have sharp boundaries. They have an internal structure with a **prototypical center** and a graded periphery. The *robin* is a more *prototypical* bird than the penguin — not because the penguin is not a bird, but because the robin concentrates more of the attributes people associate with the typical bird: it flies, it sings, it is medium-sized, it nests in trees. The penguin belongs to the category, but is far from the center.

Rosch introduces two fundamental technical concepts. **Typicality** measures how representative a member is of the category. **Feature salience** measures how informative certain attributes are for distinguishing that category from others. And categories have *levels*: the **basic** level — *bird*, *chair*, *walk* — is the cognitively privileged one, the one children learn first, to which people respond most quickly. The **superordinate** level — *animal*, *furniture*, *action* — aggregates; the **subordinate** level — *robin*, *kitchen chair*, *fast walking* — specializes.

This structure is not merely a psychological description. It is a computational principle. If concepts are organized by distance from the prototype, conceptual navigation can be formalized as traversal of a weighted graph — where the weights represent distance from the center and the strength of relationships between categories.

It is worth pausing on one limitation: Rosch herself, over time, cautioned against an overly rigid interpretation of prototypical structure. Prototypes are not fixed entities: they are sensitive to context, culture, and individual experience. An architecture based on prototypes should account for this — and this is one of the points where the hypothesis described here is weakest.

## Berlin, Kay, and the structure of the field

In 1969, anthropologist **Brent Berlin** and linguist **Paul Kay** published [*Basic Color Terms: Their Universality and Evolution*](https://www.ucpress.edu/book/9780520019645/basic-color-terms), a work that would change comparative linguistics. The question was simple: do the world's languages name colors arbitrarily, or do they follow a universal structure?

Analyzing a broad sample of highly diverse languages, they found that basic color terms follow an almost universal evolutionary hierarchy. Every language with only two color terms uses one for light and one for dark. If it has three, the third is red. If it has four, it is green or yellow. Certain color categories — red, yellow, green, blue — are, according to this theory, *focal colors*: cognitive focal points that cultures tend to converge on naming, even when their languages do not yet formally distinguish them.

This discovery is in productive tension with the **Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis** — or more precisely with its *strong* version, linguistic determinism: the idea that the structure of language determines thought. Formulated by [Edward Sapir](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/linguistic-relativity/) and developed by **Benjamin Lee Whorf** in the 1930s and 1940s, in its extreme form it implies that different languages generate incommensurable thoughts. Modern empirical studies have found real but non-deterministic *soft* effects. **Lera Boroditsky**, in [*Does Language Shape Thought?* (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-0277(01)00160-4) and in a more accessible synthesis in [*Scientific American* (2011)](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-language-shapes-thought/), showed that language influences certain cognitive processes without completely determining them. We are not prisoners of our language's categories, but we are modulated by them.

The central contribution of Berlin and Kay to this hypothesis is the notion of **field** that their research introduces. A conceptual field is not simply the set of members of a category: it is a semantic space with an oriented extension, which has a center and boundaries that are not arbitrary. The *focal colors* demonstrate precisely this: languages do not draw the boundaries between colors wherever they wish. They draw them around the cognitively most stable focal points — those that cultures converge on naming even starting from very different linguistic systems. Red has a center that all languages recognize, even those that do not yet have a word for red as a distinct category. That center is the focal point of the field, and the field's boundaries orient themselves around it.

This principle extends well beyond colors. Where does *sadness* end and *pain* begin? Where does *bird* end and *flying animal* begin? The boundaries are not traceable arbitrarily: they orient toward the focal points of adjacent categories, in a way that mirrors widely shared cognitive structures. The margins remain fuzzy, but the centers around which they organize are stable. It is this trans-linguistic stability of focal points — not merely the existence of categories — that is Berlin and Kay's specific contribution: cognitive categories have a partially universal field structure, which language can amplify or blur but cannot freely redraw.

## The symbol as gateway: Augustine, Peirce, and Manzoni

Before moving to the technical architecture — however rudimentary — it is worth pausing on a philosophical question: what does a symbol do? Not in the elementary semiotic sense — it refers to something else — but in the deep cognitive sense: *how does it lead to knowledge?*

**Augustine of Hippo**, in the [*De Magistro*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1702.htm) (389 CE) and the *De Doctrina Christiana*, develops a theory of the sign in which the *signum* is something that carries the mind beyond itself toward what it signifies. Augustine's position is radical: no external sign truly teaches. Teaching occurs only when the sign activates in the reader's mind the contemplation of the thing itself — a form of inner illumination. The symbol is a trigger, not a container. It does not carry meaning within itself: it produces the conditions for meaning to emerge.

**Charles Sanders Peirce** constructs, centuries later, the most rigorous semiotics in the history of philosophy. In his tripartition — [icon, index, symbol](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce-semiotics/) — the symbol is the sign that refers to its object by arbitrary convention, not by resemblance (icon) or causal contiguity (index). But the decisive thing in Peirce is **unlimited semiosis**: every sign produces an *interpretant* — a new sign that clarifies it — which in turn produces another interpretant, in a potentially infinite chain of mediations. Meaning is not a fixed point: it is a process, a traversal. To know something means to traverse that chain long enough to stabilize it locally.

**Alessandro Manzoni** arrives at the same concern from a different direction, and with a nearly opposite answer. In his work on the Italian language — particularly in *Della lingua italiana* and in the long engagement on the language question that culminated in his 1868 report to the ministerial commission — Manzoni sought a **univocal and social** relationship between sign and meaning. For him, the perfect symbol is one that creates no ambiguity: a word must have a shared, socially stabilized meaning, not interpretable in divergent ways. Ambiguity is a defect, not a feature. This position is almost the antipode of Peirce — and the tension between the two is productive: where Peirce sees unlimited semiosis as the very structure of meaning, Manzoni sees control of ambiguity as the condition of effective communication.

For the hypothesis this article explores, both offer something. From Peirce comes the idea that retrieving a concept is a traversal of a network of references, not direct access to a definition. From Manzoni comes the idea that the nodes of that network — the basic concepts — should have referential stability: they must not be arbitrarily reinterpretable, on pain of losing any possibility of structured reasoning.

## Theoretical foundations: a synthesis

Before moving to the architecture, it is worth pausing to gather the threads. The philosophical and cognitive traditions described in the preceding sections are not erudite ornamentation: they are the bricks from which the proposal is built. Each answers a specific question, and together they form a coherent framework.

**Plato** poses the fundamental question: does there exist a level of representation that is abstract with respect to instances, stable with respect to perceptual variation, and accessible through recognition rather than enumeration? The Platonic answer is metaphysical — the Forms of the Hyperuranion — and is not directly transferable into a computational architecture. But the structural intuition remains: a child who has seen ten horses does not memorize ten images, but constructs something stable that they use to recognize the eleventh. What that something is, is exactly the question the following sections seek to answer.

**Rosch** answers with internal structure. Concepts are not sets of necessary and sufficient conditions: they have a prototypical center and a graded periphery. The robin is a more prototypical bird than the penguin because it concentrates more of the attributes associated with the category. This structure is computationally useful: distance from the prototype can be formalized as a weight, and the weight can guide inclusion decisions during navigation. Rosch provides the structure of the *node*.

**Berlin and Kay** answer with external structure. Concepts not only have a center: they have a field with boundaries oriented toward the cognitive focal points of adjacent categories. Those boundaries are not arbitrary — they are stable across different cultures, regardless of the number of terms available. The boundary between "green" and "blue," between "sadness" and "pain," between "bird" and "flying animal" orients toward the focal points of the categories that touch. Berlin and Kay provide the structure of the *field*: not how a concept is made from the inside, but how far it extends. In an architecture that navigates a graph, this translates into a filter on the traversal's expansion — field boundaries decide where navigation stops, not only lexical matching.

The two contributions operate on distinct planes and complement each other. Rosch's *prototypicality* is a weight on the node: it governs the threshold at which a member is included — the closer to the center, the lower the threshold. Berlin and Kay's boundaries are a filter on expansion: they govern where the traversal halts. A system using only Rosch would have centers but arbitrary boundaries. One using only Berlin and Kay would have boundaries but no internal weighting mechanism. Both are needed.

**Augustine** adds a clarification about the nature of the symbol: it is not a container of meaning, but a trigger. The symbol produces the conditions for meaning to emerge — it does not carry it within itself. This distinguishes the graph node from a lexical definition: the node does not *contain* the concept, it *activates* the traversal that constructs it in response to the specific query.

**Peirce** formalizes the traversal itself. Unlimited semiosis — every sign produces an interpretant that refers to another — describes exactly the structure of conceptual navigation in a network. To know something means to traverse that chain long enough to stabilize it locally. In the hypothesized system, the stopping criterion is structural — exhaustion of the activated instances — not semantic: the chain does not stop because "enough has been understood," it stops because there are no more open paths to explore.

**Manzoni** introduces the opposite and necessary constraint: the network's nodes must have referential stability. Peirce's unlimited semiosis, without an anchor, produces a system that does not reason — it drifts. The graph's basic concepts must be univocal and not arbitrarily reinterpretable, on pain of losing any possibility of structured reasoning. It is the tension between Peirce and Manzoni — semiosis as an open process, reference as a stable anchor — that defines the structure of the composite symbol described in the proposal.

The proposal that follows does not invent an architecture from scratch: it takes these tools, combines them, and asks whether the combination produces something constructible and different from what currently exists.

## The proposal: an architecture for conceptual navigation

What follows is, to reiterate, a hypothesis formulated from the outside. It is not a technical blueprint: it is a sketch of how a system different from current LLMs might work, motivated by the theoretical traditions described above. The question is not "how is it built?" — which cannot be answered here — but "does it make sense to think in these terms?"

### The basic structure: the graph

The fundamental unit is not a word-token but a **prototypical conceptual node**: a structure that corresponds to a concept in Rosch's sense — a centroid of a semantic field, defined by distance from the examples that populate it and by the features that distinguish it from adjacent nodes.

The graph is directed and weighted. Each edge represents a relationship — hyper/hyponymy (bird → animal), meronymy (wing → bird), causality, experiential contiguity, contrast — and has a weight encoding the strength of that relationship. The hierarchical structure mirrors Rosch's levels: basic-level nodes as the primary entry point, superordinate nodes for aggregation, subordinate nodes for specialization.

Each node has, hypothetically, four components:

- **A vector centroid**: its position in semantic space, calculated as the weighted average of the examples that populate it.
- **A set of salient features**: the attributes that distinguish it from adjacent nodes, in the sense of Rosch's typicality and Berlin & Kay's informativeness.
- **A hierarchical level**: basic, superordinate, or subordinate.
- **An example-symbol**: described in detail in the following section.

### How a node is born: clustering, prototype, and symbol

Before describing the symbol, it is worth clarifying how a node forms — because the symbol is the final product of a process, not a label assigned from the outside.

The hypothesis is that nodes are built during a **top-down supervised training phase**: not pure unsupervised learning, nor completely human-directed training, but an intermediate path in which the system aggregates and clusters autonomously, and the human operator validates, corrects, or rejects the proposed groupings. This is structurally different from how current LLMs operate, whose training runs on enormous corpora in a largely automatic and step-by-step uninspectable way. Here, supervision is an integral part of the process, and this is — at least in principle — the condition that makes the system correctable at the root.

The node formation process unfolds in four phases:

**1. Clustering.** The system aggregates material — text, images, audio, structured data — by statistical association, identifying co-occurrence clusters. This is already well-understood territory: clustering techniques such as k-means, DBSCAN, or community detection algorithms on graphs are established tools. The difference from standard usage is that clustering occurs on multimodal material with the explicit goal of producing conceptual nodes, not categories for classification.

**2. Selection of the focal point and periphery.** Within each cluster, the system identifies the prototypical centroid — the statistically most representative point, in Rosch's sense — and determines the periphery by graded distance from that center. Not all members of the cluster carry equal weight: the robin is closer to the center of *bird* than the penguin, and this differential distance is explicitly encoded in the node. The human supervisor can intervene at this stage: moving the center, expanding or contracting the periphery, splitting a cluster into two distinct nodes, or merging two overly similar clusters.

**3. Determination of extension.** The system evaluates how far the cluster extends relative to adjacent clusters — where *bird* ends and *flying animal* begins, where *sadness* ends and *pain* begins. Here Berlin & Kay's criteria apply: boundaries are not arbitrary, but orient toward the most stable cognitive focal points. Margins remain fuzzy, as Rosch teaches, but the system makes them explicit rather than leaving them implicit in the statistics.

**4. Derivation of the symbol.** Once the cluster is established — center, periphery, extension, relations with adjacent nodes — the system generates an identifying symbol. The format is not predefined rigidly: it emerges from the statistical characteristics of the cluster itself, with human supervision during training. If the cluster is dominated by visual data with a stable form, the symbol will incorporate an SVG or parametric 3D model that embodies the prototypical properties — not a photograph of an instance, but a form that condenses what makes the entity recognizable. If the cluster is dominated by temporal patterns or state transitions — an action, a process — the symbol will incorporate a sequential schema. If the cluster corresponds to an experiential or abstract concept for which no direct visual representation is adequate, the representational component will be arbitrary in the Manzonian sense: stable, univocal, unambiguous — an identifier that claims to depict nothing, but guarantees access to the node in a non-duplicable way, analogous to a [Symbol in JavaScript (ES2020)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Symbol).

The symbol is arbitrary in its representational format, but not random: it is the compressed product of the statistically most representative characteristics of the cluster. Human supervision does not choose the symbol: it validates or rejects the system's choice, intervening when the generated symbol does not adequately capture the node's structure.

### The composite structure of the symbol

The symbol is not a simple object. It is a structure with two superimposed layers, where the stable layer constitutes the foundation and the episodic layer rests upon it without modifying it. Both deserve precise description, because this structure is what distinguishes the symbol from a simple label or static embedding.

**Stable layer — built in training, invariant in use.** Contains everything that defines the node permanently:

- The **node's UUID** (Universally Unique Identifier): a unique identifier generated at the moment of the node's creation, never modifiable and never reassigned. The UUID is the operational fulcrum of the entire architecture — the point upon which the entire system anchors itself in order to operate without ambiguity.

  Within the system, every operation involving a node occurs by UUID: navigation during the fetch, resolution of structural relations, episodic layer updates, construction of the RAG. The system does not use text labels or graph positions as operational references — it uses UUIDs. Two nodes with the same label in different graphs, or in different versions of the same graph, have distinct UUIDs and cannot be confused.

  Outside the system, the UUID is the access key for anyone connecting to the graph from the outside: authorized graphs, dedicated parsers, indexing systems, human supervision tools. A node is not referenced by name — it is referenced by UUID. In the episodic layer, connections do not link labels: they link UUIDs. When an episodic connection is "touched" during a fetch, it is the UUID that provides immediate access to the node and the data it contains. This property is essential: connections accumulated over time remain valid even if the node's content is updated or its label changes, because they are tied to the node's identity, not its description.

- The **perceptual representation**: the parametric 3D model, the prototypical SVG, the sequential schema, or the arbitrary identifier — depending on the nature of the cluster.
- The **link to the belonging graph**: the reference to the graph (the system's own, or an authorized external one) in which the node resides, necessary for navigation and for parsers dedicated to external graphs.
- The **prototypical centroid**: the node's position in semantic space, with the typicality weights of the examples that populate it — in Rosch's sense.
- The **categorical field**: the node's extension relative to adjacent nodes, determined according to Berlin & Kay's criteria — where the concept begins and where it ends, relative to the most stable cognitive focal points.
- The **structural relations**: the edges toward other nodes in the graph — hyper/hyponymy, meronymy, causality, contrast — with their fixed weights. Each relation references the destination node by UUID, not by text label.

**Episodic layer — append-only, accumulated in use.** Contains the contextual connections that emerge during navigation, which do not belong to the node's stable semantic structure but to its history of use. Each connection recorded in this layer carries:

- The **UUID of the connected symbol**: not the name or label of the other symbol, but its UUID. This guarantees that the link remains valid and unambiguous over time, regardless of any updates to the connected node's label or content. When the fetch traverses this connection, the UUID provides direct access to all the connected node's data.
- The **field of intersection**: in which semantic area — in Rosch's and Berlin & Kay's sense — the two symbols met. If *dead* and *laughing* met in the area of extreme physical experience, that is the extent of the recorded intersection, not the totality of both fields.
- The **nature of the link**: whether it is a direct intersection between instances, a connection mediated by an intermediate node (Chrysippus, in the example below), or a link toward an external graph.

This structure has an important operational consequence: when the fetch encounters a symbol with already-recorded episodic connections, it can use them as shortcuts — finding more quickly intersections that in a previous navigation already demonstrated their relevance. The episodic layer does not replace the traversal: it orients it.

The overall symbol's function, in Augustine's terms, remains that of a trigger: it is a **grounding** — the perceptual and categorical anchor of the node — and a **trigger** — the access point for the traversal, usable from linguistic, visual, or structured inputs, and progressively enriched through use.

The distinction between the two layers corresponds to the one cognitive psychology draws between **semantic memory** — the stable structure of concepts, independent of episodes — and **episodic memory** — the contextual connections accumulated through experience. The graph is the system's semantic memory; the episodic layer of the symbols is its episodic memory. Both coexist without interfering, because they operate on distinct objects: the graph is never modified by use, only the episodic layer of the symbols grows.

An honest clarification: the composite structure described is conceptually coherent, and its separate components — multimodal clustering, prototypical embeddings, conditionally generated representations, two-level storage — are existing technologies. What does not yet exist is their integration into a pipeline where the composite symbol is structurally bound to the node, functions as an access point for traversal, and accumulates episodic connections robustly. The distance between available components and the integrated system is real.

### The progressive fetch

The **progressive fetch** is the mechanism by which the system navigates the graph to construct a response. It does not retrieve a pre-compiled entry and does not explore everything connected to a symbol: it *traverses* the network iteratively, guided by the specific instances activated by the query, until those instances are resolved.

A point worth clarifying before describing the process: the fetch does not look for everything connected to a concept — it looks for everything necessary to answer that specific query. "I died laughing" activates *dead* and *laughing* as instances; the fetch expands from those two, not from the entire semantic field of death or laughter. The stopping criterion is not semantic in the vague sense of "when I have enough": it is **structural**, tied to the exhaustion of the activated instances. When the open instances are exhausted, there is nothing else to search.

The process unfolds in a sequence worth describing with precision, because it is the heart of the architecture:

**Phase 1 — Translating input into instances.** The input — linguistic, visual, or structured — is processed and translated into the graph instances that correspond to it: the symbols activated by the specific query. An exact match is not sought: the most pertinent entry nodes are identified, preferably at the basic level in Rosch's sense, because those are the cognitively privileged and computationally most accessible ones. For "I died laughing," the instances are *dead* and *laughing*. Each instance is an autonomous starting point for the traversal.

**Phase 2 — Iterative fetch by inclusion and exclusion.** Starting from each instance, the system traverses adjacent edges evaluating the relevance of each encountered node relative to the query. Pertinent nodes are included in the pool; non-pertinent ones are excluded and the traversal does not follow them further. Each instance is an autonomous search: it navigates, collects, synthesizes. The system can find, expanding from *dead* and *laughing*, the anecdote of Chrysippus — who allegedly died laughing at one of his own jokes — as an intersection between the two fields. That connection did not exist in the structural graph: it emerges from the joint navigation of the two instances.

**Phase 3 — Systematization of the RAG and elimination of the superfluous.** When all instances have been resolved, the system organizes the collected pool into a structured RAG — ordered by relevance, prototypical hierarchy, and distance from the center of the activated nodes — and eliminates nodes that prove redundant or peripheral relative to the specific query. The result is not a raw set of data: it is a structured context, already filtered, ready for the upper layer.

**Phase 4 — Updating the episodic layer of the symbols.** Once the fetch is complete and the pool is built, the episodic layer of the entry instances' symbols is updated to record the connections that emerged during traversal. If the navigation linked *dead* and *laughing* through Chrysippus, that connection is not written into the structural graph — which remains unchanged — but is added to the episodic layer of both symbols. The recorded link carries: the UUID of the connected symbol (not its name — its unique identifier, which remains valid even if the node's label were to change), the field of intersection (the semantic area in which the two concepts met, in the sense of Rosch and Berlin & Kay), and the nature of the link (direct intersection, mediated, or link toward an external graph). This is not an update of the model's parameters: it is a discrete, localized, inspectable, and reversible addendum. Future queries that activate the same instances will find that link already available as a shortcut — the system reads the UUID in the episodic layer, accesses the connected node directly, and includes it in the pool without having to rediscover it through traversal.

This mechanism is structurally different from the fine-tuning of neural parameters: it does not modify continuous weights, it adds discrete references. It does not suffer from catastrophic forgetting because adding a link to a symbol does not overwrite anything existing. And it is inspectable: every accumulated connection is an explicit object, not an adjustment distributed across millions of parameters.

The analogy with Peirce's semiosis remains pertinent for describing the traversal: each activated node is an interpretant that refers to other interpretant-nodes. The difference from Peirce — important — is that in this system the chain terminates when instances are exhausted, not according to a semantic criterion that is difficult to formalize. It is a structural criterion, not an arbitrary one.

### Symbols as bridges between graphs

One aspect of the fetch that deserves a separate note concerns the system's behavior when traversal reaches the boundaries of the system's own graph — the nodes that face external authorized graphs.

At that point, the boundary node's symbol is not only an internal identifier: it becomes a **link toward the external graph**. This link is anchored to the boundary node's UUID: it is the UUID that the dedicated parser receives as a key, and uses to know to which node in the internal graph the externally retrieved data must be attributed. The system does not incorporate the external graph's nodes as its own: it references them through the bridge symbol's UUID, reads their relations through a dedicated parser — built specifically for that source, by the human who authorized that graph — and reports the connections into the RAG pool as readable data, associated with the UUID of the node that generated them. Once in the pool, data from different graphs can be compared by the upper layer's LLM on a common basis: their ontological heterogeneity has been resolved upstream, at the moment of navigation, not at the moment of output.

This solution has a precise technical advantage: the problem of alignment between different ontologies — which in the previous version of this hypothesis was an open limitation — is handled locally, source by source, with dedicated parsers and human supervision over the selection of sources. It does not require a preventive universal normalization, which would be unrealistic; it requires a specific translation mechanism for each authorized graph, which is a standard engineering problem. The UUID ensures that this translation remains traceable: every piece of data in the pool knows which node it came from, and that node is always identifiable unambiguously.

### The intermediate layer: structured RAG instead of tensor algebra

The initial version of this hypothesis described the intermediate layer as a system of algebraic operations between vectors — tensor products that combine nodes to produce composite representations. That formalization is theoretically coherent, and Smolensky described it rigorously with [*Tensor Product Representations*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702(90)90143-Y) (1990). The problem is that it does not scale: the dimensionality of composite spaces grows combinatorially as the number of combined concepts increases, and attempts to apply it to general systems have not produced convincing results.

A more pragmatic formulation — and probably closer to something buildable — replaces the tensor product with a **structured RAG produced by progressive fetch**. The intermediate layer does not calculate a composite vector: it collects, filters, and orders a set of nodes through the traversal described above, and passes it to the upper layer as structured context.

The difference from a conventional RAG lies in the structure of the produced context. A conventional RAG retrieves textual chunks by superficial vector similarity. The RAG produced by progressive fetch is ordered by prototypical relevance, conceptual hierarchy, and distance from the center of the activated nodes — it is an already-interpreted context, not merely retrieved. The LLM receiving it does not work on raw text: it works on a circumscribed pool in which the problem's structure is already partially resolved.

It is worth being explicit about what this changes relative to tensor algebra, and what it does not. What changes is feasibility: the structured RAG is buildable with existing technologies, the scalable tensor product is not. What does not change is the fundamental limit: a RAG retrieves what is already structured in the graph, while the tensor product could — in theory — produce representations of concepts not yet present as nodes. In practice, however, the iterative fetch partially compensates: by expanding progressively through the network and recording new connections, it can build by incremental composition what does not exist as a single node, even if in a less compact way than a true algebraic operation.

### The system's layers

The hypothesized architecture has three distinct layers, with separate functions.

**Layer 1 — The conceptual graph** is the permanent structure of prototypical nodes, their symbols, and their relations. It is not modified during ordinary use: it is the system's long-term memory. It is updated only when the progressive fetch records new connections between nodes, and that update occurs on the topology — new edges — not on the model's parameters.

Its construction does not start from scratch. Conceptual graphs of significant size already exist: [ConceptNet](https://conceptnet.io/), [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/), [FrameNet](https://framenet.icsi.berkeley.edu/), domain ontologies built over decades. The hypothesis is that the system hooks into these existing structures, adding the prototypical dimension — centroids, typicality weights, symbols — that these structures lack. The system's own graph would be relatively compact: a stable core of basic concepts, plus connections toward external graphs selected and authorized by the human.

The expansion is not uniform. Basic concepts — *horse*, *run*, *above*, *after* — do not require continuous updating. Selective expansion focuses on complex, contextual, or emergent concepts, where knowledge changes and where human supervision is most effective.

**Layer 2 — Structured retrieval** is the progressive fetch mechanism described in the previous section. Its output is not an algebraic composite representation: it is a **structured RAG** — a pool of nodes ordered by prototypical relevance, conceptual hierarchy, and distance from the center of the activated nodes, already filtered through the elimination of the superfluous. This pool is specific to the current query and temporary: it does not modify the graph, save for the recording of new connections that emerged.

The choice of structured RAG over tensor operations is pragmatic: tensor operations on concept vectors are theoretically coherent but not scalable to the dimensions required by a general system. The fetch-driven structured RAG is buildable with existing technologies, produces an already-interpreted context rather than raw text, and circumscribes the upper layer's work to a set of data already filtered and ordered.

**Layer 3 — The linguistic layer** is the most open component of the entire proposal, and it is worth treating it explicitly as a hypothesis, not as a defined element. Its function is to translate the pool produced by Layer 2 into comprehensible linguistic output. At least two options are plausible.

The first is to use an existing LLM: the model receives the pool as circumscribed context and generates language from it. The advantage is that it requires no training from scratch, and the already-filtered pool structurally limits the space within which the LLM can move. The residual risk is that the LLM reintroduces statistical bias into the linguistic surface, even with a precise context.

The second is a simpler solution: a system that directly processes the pool's vector representations without passing through the complexity of a general language model — a mapping from the structured output of Layer 2 to linguistic output, more direct and less exposed to statistical bias. This option is more hypothetical: it would require defining precisely what it means to "process" a structured vector pool into language, which is not straightforward.

Which of the two is preferable — or whether a third path exists — is a question that cannot be resolved from outside the field.

## An operational taxonomy — with appropriate reservations

How is the graph internally structured? An operational classification is worth proposing, with the caveat that any adopted taxonomy is a tool, not a truth. **Wittgenstein** showed in [*Philosophical Investigations*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/) that concepts do not have sharp boundaries but *family resemblances*; **Searle** and **Austin** distinguished between locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts, showing that meaning depends on the context of use more than on the internal structure of concepts. Any grid adopted is provisional.

| Level | Type | Examples | Symbol format (emerging from cluster) | Grounding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basic | Perceptual entity | horse, apple, table | Prototypical SVG or parametric 3D model | High |
| Basic | Simple action | walk, cut, fall | State transition schema, sequential pattern | High |
| Superordinate | Abstract category | animal, action, object | Aggregative symbol — often relational SVG | Medium |
| Subordinate | Specific instance | robin, running fast | Detailed SVG, close to the instance | Very high |
| Experiential | Emergent concept | war, love, loneliness | Stable arbitrary identifier — no direct visual form | Low — emerges through intersection |
| Structural | Pure relation | above, inside, after | Abstract spatial schema, directed diagram | Medium |

*Experiential* concepts — war, love, injustice — are those for which the progressive fetch is both most necessary and most difficult. They have no simple prototype like the robin: they emerge from the intersection of multiple sub-networks, each activated with different weights depending on context. "To love" activates differently when the context is a romantic poem, a neurobiology treatise, or an ethical discussion. The graph does not change: what changes is the path of traversal. This is, perhaps, the hypothesis's primary strength — and also the most difficult to evaluate without an implementation.

## Where research has already arrived — and where it has not yet

The most important part of this article is this: clarifying what already exists, what is emerging, and what remains in the domain of speculation. Current research has explored many of the separate components of this hypothesis, but not their integration.

### Existing Knowledge Graphs: foundations, not a starting point from scratch

**Knowledge Graphs** are the technology closest to the described architecture — and in this hypothesis they are not merely a term of comparison, but a concrete resource to build upon. Structures such as [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/), Semantic Web ontologies ([OWL, RDF — W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/owl2-overview/)), and knowledge bases such as [ConceptNet](https://conceptnet.io/) (MIT, 2004) and [FrameNet](https://framenet.icsi.berkeley.edu/) (Berkeley, 1997) represent decades of collaborative work on the structure of knowledge. A systematic review published in *ACM Computing Surveys* by Hogan et al. — [*Knowledge Graphs* (2022)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3447772) — documents the state of the field.

The limitation of these tools relative to the hypothesis described here remains real: they are predominantly static, represent predefined relations between named entities, and lack mechanisms for computing prototypical typicality or for making abstract concepts emerge through intersection. They are relational dictionaries, not reasoning engines. But this does not make them useless: it makes them the raw material on which the system could work — hooking into already-built structures, adding the missing prototypical dimension, and establishing connections between graphs that originally did not communicate.

The technical problem this approach introduces is that of **knowledge graph alignment**: reconciling different ontologies, different levels of granularity, and internal contradictions between sources that appear coherent but are not in detail. It is a documented and active research problem — works such as [*PARIS: Probabilistic Alignment of Relations, Instances, and Schema*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.14778/2078331.2078332) (Suchanek et al., 2012) and recent reviews on *entity alignment* in heterogeneous KGs show that the distance between "having two graphs" and "having a coherent graph" is real and non-trivial. Human supervision over sources reduces the problem but does not eliminate it.

### Retrieval-Augmented Generation and GraphRAG

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation** (RAG), proposed by Lewis et al. — [*Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks*, NeurIPS 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401) — is a concrete step toward the fetch: instead of entrusting all knowledge to the model's parameters, the system retrieves relevant documents at the moment of responding. A recent variant closer to the idea described here is [**GraphRAG**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130) (Edge et al., Microsoft 2024), which integrates graph traversal into the retrieval process.

GraphRAG is probably the operational system closest to progressive fetch on a graph. It nonetheless remains distant on one fundamental point: it operates on textual chunks, not on prototypical conceptual nodes. Retrieval is guided by superficial vector similarity between texts, not by a structured traversal of a concept network with prototypical weights and algebraic operations between nodes. It is a step in the right direction, but on a different plane.

### Prototypical networks and few-shot learning

In the machine learning field, **Prototypical Networks** by Snell et al. ([NeurIPS 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.05175)) are perhaps the work most directly inspired by Rosch's theory. The idea is that each class is represented by a *prototype* — the average of the embeddings of the class's examples — and that classification occurs by distance from the nearest prototype. It works well in *few-shot learning* contexts — recognizing categories with few examples — and captures something real about Rosch's prototypical structure.

The limitation is that Prototypical Networks are classification systems, not reasoning engines on graphs. Prototypes are not nodes in a navigable network: they are centroids in an embedding space. The hierarchical structure and relations between categories are not represented.

### Neuro-symbolic AI: the most ambitious synthesis

The research thread closest to the hypothesized integration is **neuro-symbolic AI**, which attempts the synthesis between neural learning and symbolic reasoning. Garcez and Lamb, in [*Neurosymbolic AI: The 3rd Wave* (*Artificial Intelligence Review*, 2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-022-10335-w), and Hitzler et al. in [*Neuro-symbolic approaches in artificial intelligence* (*National Science Review*, 2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9166567) document a growing but still fragmented field.

The most promising systems include the [**Neural Theorem Provers**](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.05046) by Rocktäschel and Riedel (2017), which combine vector embeddings with logical rules, and the [**Logic Tensor Networks**](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04422) by Donadello et al. (2017), which represent both symbols and logical relations in vector spaces. Here too, Rosch's prototypical structure and the autonomously generated example-symbol are absent. The leap has not yet been made.

### JEPA and World Models

In 2022, **Yann LeCun** published [*A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence*](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=BZ5a1r-kVsf) — perhaps the most radical critique of autoregressive LLMs from within the AI mainstream. His proposal, **JEPA** (*Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture*), aims to build predictive representations of the world in a shared latent space, not on token probabilities. Related research on [*Energy-Based Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02572) (Dawid and LeCun, 2023) explores how to represent conceptual structures as minima of energy functions.

The convergence with the hypothesis described here is real but partial. JEPA is concerned with *prediction* in latent space, not with navigation of a prototypical graph. It remains for now primarily theoretical: a working JEPA system at real scale does not yet exist. It is worth naming it, however, as the most ambitious direction coming from within the AI community.

### The state of the field in summary

| Component | Existing technology | Status | Distance from the hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Navigable conceptual graph | ConceptNet, Wikidata, FrameNet, domain KGs | Mature | Medium — lacks prototypical dimension, but reusable |
| Graph alignment (via dedicated parsers) | Source-specific parsers, human supervision | Standard — solvable per source | Low — engineering problem, not research |
| Iterative fetch on graph with inclusion/exclusion | Beam search, early stopping, graph traversal | Consolidated technique | Medium — lacks integration with prototypical structure |
| Structured RAG as fetch output | GraphRAG (Microsoft, 2024), KG-to-text | Advanced research | Medium — lacks prototypical fetch upstream |

| Prototypical nodes | Prototypical Networks (2017) | Active research (few-shot) | Medium — not integrated in navigable graph |
| Cluster-generated symbol (supervised) | Multimodal clustering + Text-to-SVG, Shap-E | Components available | Medium — integration missing |
| Neuro-symbolic AI | NTP, LTN, DeepProbLog | Advanced research | Medium-high |

| Linguistic layer — direct vector option | Encoder-decoder on structured representations | Experimental | High — not defined for this use |

## Structural limits — those this hypothesis does not resolve, and three external objections

Naming open problems is not a rhetorical exercise in humility: it is the most useful part of an article written by someone unable to evaluate technical feasibility from the inside. To these are added three objections raised by a generative AI system during the discussion of this hypothesis — reported here, in this second article, because it is in the detail of the architecture that they find a precise answer.

### The friction between discrete and continuous

The objection is that neural networks operate in continuous and differentiable mathematical spaces — which allows backpropagation to be used for large-scale model training — while a discrete graph is not differentiable in the required sense. The objection is technically correct.

The answer is that the system described here uses differentiability where it is available and relinquishes it where it is not needed. During training, the construction of the nodes' vector centroids occurs in continuous spaces and can use backpropagation normally: that phase is neural and differentiable. During use, the traversal of the graph is discrete by deliberate choice, not out of ignorance of the problem: an edge is either traversed or it is not, and this is exactly the structure that makes reasoning inspectable and errors localizable.

The real problem that remains, and that is worth naming, is more subtle: if the graph's edge weights need to be optimized during use — not merely added, but graded — the problem of differentiability on discrete structures re-emerges. The architecture described here avoids this problem by sharply separating the two phases: weights are fixed in training, use only adds discrete links on the symbols. If one wanted a system with adaptive weights during use, the problem would reopen.

### The Symbol Grounding Problem

The objection is that anchoring nodes to multimodal data — video, images, audio — does not resolve Harnad's Symbol Grounding Problem: for a machine, a video of an apple is a tensor of RGB values, not the experience of an apple. The bio-physical foundation of experience is missing.

The objection is philosophically correct — and the article has never argued otherwise. The Symbol Grounding Problem is an open problem for any system, biological or artificial: even the photons striking the human retina are physical signals transformed into electrical impulses, not "the apple" itself. The question of what constitutes genuine experience is not resolvable by a computational architecture.

What multimodal anchoring does — and what has value — is reduce the distance between the symbol and its perceptual referents compared to a purely textual system. It does not resolve Harnad; it approximates it better than an LLM that has seen only text. The article uses "partially reduces," not "resolves": if the objection is directed at that formulation, it is correct; if it is directed at the underlying idea, it raises the bar higher than the problem requires.

### The fetch stopping criterion and combinatorial explosion

This is the most substantive of the three objections in its original formulation: in a graph weighted on a prototypical basis, everything is weakly connected to everything, and the risk is combinatorial explosion on experiential concepts like "justice."

The answer is that the stopping criterion is not semantic — it is not based on judging when "enough" has been gathered — but structural: the fetch stops when the instances activated by the query are exhausted. "I died laughing" activates *dead* and *laughing* as instances; the fetch expands from those two, not from the entire semantic space of death or laughter. When the instances are exhausted, there is nothing else to search.

This shifts the problem precisely: the risk of combinatorial explosion does not concern the traversal, but the **input-to-instances translation phase**. If that phase identifies too many initial instances, or instances that are too generic, the resulting fetch can nonetheless become broad. The quality of the response therefore depends significantly on the quality of Phase 1 — and this is the critical open point, not the traversal itself.

---

### The hypothesis's own limits

**Input-to-instances translation.** As just noted, this is the most critical phase of the entire mechanism. For ambiguous, polysemous, or poorly posed queries, identifying the correct instances is non-trivial. An error in this phase produces a correct fetch but on wrong premises — and the resulting pool, however structured, answers a different question from the one posed. How to calibrate this phase robustly across heterogeneous queries is probably the architecture's primary implementation challenge.

**Fetch scalability on large graphs.** Iterative traversal with delimited instances is computationally far more manageable than the exhaustive alternative. On very large graphs, with connections toward external graphs, complexity depends on the number of activated instances and the density of the traversed areas — it is difficult to estimate without an implementation.

**Grounding of the generated symbol.** The symbol derivation process is conceptually non-circular. The problem that remains open is integration: the symbol must be structurally bound to the node in a way that allows the system to use it as a traversal trigger, not merely as a label. The separate components exist; the integrated pipeline does not.

**The linguistic layer as an open choice.** The exact form of the output layer remains indeterminate. An existing LLM receiving the circumscribed pool is the most pragmatic option; a more direct vector system is more hypothetical. The choice affects the residual risk of statistical bias in the output. It cannot be resolved from outside the field.

**Computational cost.** The reuse of existing graphs and the replacement of the tensor product with structured RAG reduce the cost relative to the original version of the hypothesis. How much remains, and whether it is compatible with conventional hardware, is difficult to estimate without a prototype.

## An open hypothesis, not a blueprint

The proposal this article has sought to explore is not a system realizable in the immediate term. It is a structural intuition — that meaning emerges from navigating a network, not from the statistical compression of a corpus — which finds roots in diverse philosophical traditions and points of contact with existing research, without coinciding with any of them.

The theoretical roots hold: Rosch's prototypical structure offers a cognitive formalization of nodes, Berlin and Kay offer a theory of the trans-linguistic structure of categories, Peirce offers a model of meaning as traversal, Manzoni offers a constraint of referential stability for symbols. These are not technical foundations: they are conceptual motivations for asking whether an alternative architecture makes sense.

The technical components exist, in forms different from those needed but not incompatible with them: Knowledge Graphs to reuse and enrich with prototypical structure, mechanisms for iterative traversal on graphs, structured RAG as fetch output technology, GraphRAG as a precedent for retrieval on graphs, neuro-symbolic AI as an attempt at synthesis between navigation and reasoning. None is directly applicable as a whole; all are material to build from.

The evolution of the hypothesis over the course of this article has made the proposal more concrete on some points and more honest on others. Replacing the tensor product with the fetch-driven structured RAG has made the intermediate layer buildable with existing technologies. The clarification on iterative fetch by inclusion and exclusion has dissolved the problem of infinite RAG. The bridge-symbol solution has reduced the problem of graph alignment from an open question to a per-source engineering problem. What remains open — the fetch stopping criterion, the exact form of the linguistic layer, the integration of the symbol as a traversal trigger — are localized problems, not principled obstacles.

Whether the integration of these components is technically achievable, in what timeframe and with what architectures, is a question that honestly has no answer today — and one that probably cannot receive an answer from whoever formulates it from outside the field. What can be said is that the distance between this hypothesis and something prototypable has decreased over the course of its elaboration, and that the questions it poses — how does one separate conceptual navigation from linguistic generation? how does one build a circumscribed context instead of a statistical response? how does one make reasoning errors localizable? — are questions that current research is attempting to address, without yet a unified answer.
