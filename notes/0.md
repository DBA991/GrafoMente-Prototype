# A note before reading

This series of articles is not a research paper, a technical proposal, or a product announcement. It is something more modest and, I think, more honest: a set of exploratory notes written by someone who does not work in the field of artificial intelligence, does not speak its specific language, and has no institutional affiliation with it.

The ideas developed here emerged while studying how large language models work — reading papers, documentation, and technical discussions, and trying to understand not just the mechanics but the structural assumptions underneath them. The articles are the trace of that process: thinking in writing, with all the approximations that implies.

---

## How these articles were written

The drafting process was unconventional. The initial ideas were developed through extended conversations with AI systems — primarily Gemini (Google), Claude (Anthropic), and DeepSeek (High-Flyer) — used as interlocutors for brainstorming: to stress-test intuitions, surface objections, find the right terminology, and identify where the reasoning was weak. The written articles are the product of those conversations, reorganized, refined, and verified by the author.

The prototype application described in the fourth article was built with AI assistance and tested in a sandboxed environment at the time of its creation.

This is stated not as a disclaimer about AI involvement — which is visible throughout and not a concern — but because it is relevant to how the ideas should be read: as the output of a dialogue, not of independent technical research. The central proposals in each article are the author's own, in the sense that they represent positions the author reached and endorses. They are not guaranteed to be correct.

---

## What these articles are

**Exploratory hypotheses.** The core idea — an AI that navigates a graph of prototypical concepts instead of statistically compressing a text corpus — is a speculative direction, not a demonstrated result. The prototype shows that the flow works mechanically on a small domain. It does not show that the architecture scales, that it outperforms existing systems in controlled benchmarks, or that it solves problems that current RAG systems cannot.

**Honest about limits.** Each article tries to separate what is demonstrated from what is inferred, what exists from what is proposed, and what is technically sound from what remains open. Where the reasoning is weakest, the articles say so. The gap between the theoretical architecture and the working prototype is documented explicitly in the accompanying repository files.

**Written for exposure, not validation.** The reason for publishing is to put the idea in front of people who know the field better than the author does — to find out whether the intuition points at something real, whether the problems it tries to solve are the right problems, and whether the proposed mechanisms have analogues or contradictions in existing research that the author has not found.

---

## What to expect

The four articles in the series build on each other:

1. **Towards a Digital *Characteristica Universalis*** — the philosophical starting point: why ideographic representation might be a more useful model for AI than phonetic token prediction, and what the research landscape around World Models and structured retrieval looks like.

2. **The Graph as Mind** — the architecture in detail: prototypical nodes, the two-layer composite symbol, progressive fetch by instances, and the theoretical traditions (Rosch, Berlin & Kay, Peirce, Augustine, Manzoni) that motivate each choice.

3. **From Graph to Code** — the application of the architecture to code generation, the case for DeepSeek as a linguistic layer, and the available technology stack.

4. **GrafoMente: A Prototype** — the working implementation, what it demonstrates, and what it does not.

The repository also contains two additional documents: a `README.md` describing how to run the prototype, and an `ARCHITECTURE.md` mapping the gap between the implemented prototype and the complete architecture, including future directions.
