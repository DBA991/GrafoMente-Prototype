# Towards a Digital *Characteristica Universalis*: Ideograms, Vectors, and the Structural Limits of Artificial Intelligence

*A philosophical and technical exploration of AI's future — between Leibniz's dream and World Model architectures*

---

## First came speech, then writing

Everything started with a simple question: did humans learn to write before they learned to speak?

The answer is obvious, but the timescale is striking. Scientific estimates place the earliest forms of complex language between **200,000 and 50,000 years ago**, while writing only emerges around **3,200 BCE** in Mesopotamia, with the Sumerians. The ratio is at least 1 to 40: for every year of writing, there are forty years in which humans relied exclusively on the spoken word.

As theorized by **Walter J. Ong** in *[Orality and Literacy](https://www.routledge.com/Orality-and-Literacy/Ong/p/book/9780415281294)* (1982), the spoken word is a biological and dynamic event, while writing is a technology — something constructed, learned, transmitted. The pictographic and ideographic systems that preceded it — in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China — were not transcriptions of sounds: they were administrative management tools, the first form of "external memory" for complex data.

| Phase | Period | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Oral Language | > 150,000 years ago | Complex sonic communication |
| Pictograms | ~40,000 years ago | Graphic representation of real objects |
| Ideograms | ~5,500 years ago | Signs representing concepts and ideas |
| Phonetic Alphabet | ~3,500 years ago | Signs representing individual sounds (Phoenicians) |

It is worth pausing on a distinction that is often overlooked. Pictograms and ideograms are not writing in the phonetic sense: they do not transcribe sounds, but **represent concepts directly**. A pictogram of a bison means *bison* regardless of the language spoken by whoever looks at it; a Chinese ideogram for "sadness" has no intrinsic pronunciation — it has meaning. The phonetic alphabet, by contrast, is a system for encoding sound: letters have no meaning on their own, acquiring it only when arranged in sequence according to the rules of a specific language.

This difference is not merely historical. It is structural, and it returns to center stage when reasoning about how machines process language — and how they might do so differently.

## Leibniz's dream

Before reasoning about the present, it is worth stepping back — to 1666.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published the *[Dissertatio de Arte Combinatoria](https://www.leibniz-translationen.de/)*. His vision was radical: build a **universal alphabet of human thoughts**, where every concept was represented by a primitive symbol and reasoning became pure calculation — *Calculemus*. Thomas Hobbes, in the *[Leviathan](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3207)* (1651), had already argued something similar: reasoning is nothing but reckoning. Two philosophers, one shared intuition — thought has an underlying algebraic structure.

This intuition remained in the domain of philosophy for centuries. Today, in the architecture of AI systems, it is becoming an engineering question.

## How an AI reads — and why that isn't enough

Modern Large Language Models do not read letters or sounds. They read **tokens** — text units that may correspond to whole words or parts of them — and transform them into **vectors**, sequences of numbers encoding semantic relationships. This process is called **word embedding**, formalized by Tomas Mikolov et al. in *[Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781)* (2013). The classical example from the literature:

> **King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen**

Vectors do not encode the sounds of words, but their semantic relationships in mathematical space — they already approximate an ideographic system more than a phonetic one, in the sense that they capture relative meaning, not pronunciation. It is worth not overstating the analogy, however: current vectors capture statistical co-occurrences in text, not concepts in the philosophical sense. They are a starting point, not a destination.

The deeper problem is another, and it is structural. It is called the **Symbol Grounding Problem**, formulated by Stevan Harnad in *[The Symbol Grounding Problem](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/9906002)* (1990, *Physica D*, [DOI](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0167278990900876)): how can a system that manipulates symbols acquire intrinsic meaning, rather than depending on the meanings present in the minds of the humans who built it? Harnad's analogy is effective: it is like trying to learn Chinese using only a Chinese-Chinese dictionary. A structure of symbolic cross-references accumulates, but the real world is never touched.

For an AI trained on text, the problem manifests like this: the word "apple" is statistically linked to other words — fruit, sweet, red — but the AI has never bitten an apple, never felt its weight or smelled its scent. Understanding remains parasitic on human experience encoded in text. And it is precisely here that the hypothesis I want to explore takes shape.

## The proposal: navigating concepts instead of memorizing words

The core idea is this: an AI could be structured around **composite vector symbols** — not phonetic text — partially anchored to real experiential data such as video, audio, and images, generating phonetic text only as output. But the most important part is not the representation: it is the mechanism by which the system moves between concepts, and the internal structure of the symbols themselves.

In current models, training is essentially **retention**: the model compresses billions of parameters mirroring the statistical co-occurrences of the entire corpus. The result is a system that does not "retrieve" information — it **reconstructs it statistically** each time, with all the margins of error this entails. This is the source of hallucinations: the model produces the most plausible token given the context, without a verifiable anchor to the original data.

The alternative hypothesized here is training as **traversal**, not retention. The AI would navigate the data to extract its conceptual structure, position each concept within the network, and build symbols encoding its properties. The system's "memory" would not be a compressed copy of the corpus, but the **correct location of each concept in a graph** — a network of connected nodes, each positioned in relation to the others.

---

## The structure of symbols: stable at the foundation, open in use

The point where this proposal diverges most sharply from existing systems concerns the nature of the symbols associated with each conceptual node. These are not labels or simple identifiers: each symbol is a **composite structure with two superimposed layers**.

The stable layer — built during training and invariant in use — contains, first of all, a **UUID** (Universally Unique Identifier) assigned to the node at the moment of its creation and never modified. This identifier is the operational key of the entire architecture: every reference to a node, both internal to the system and external to it, passes through its UUID. The system can refer to nodes unambiguously using the UUID; similarly, in the episodic layer, every connection does not link labels or positions in the graph — it links UUIDs. When an episodic connection is "touched" during a fetch, it is the UUID that provides immediate access to the node and the data it contains.

Beyond the UUID, the stable layer contains the perceptual representation of the concept (a 3D model or SVG for physical entities, a sequential schema for actions, a stable arbitrary identifier for abstract concepts), the node's position in semantic space according to Rosch's prototype theory, the categorical boundaries oriented by Berlin and Kay's focal points, and the structural relationships with adjacent nodes. This is the system's semantic memory: stable, verifiable, unmodifiable during use.

The episodic layer rests on this foundation without modifying it. It accumulates during use and records the contextual connections that emerge from navigation — each connection identifies the connected node via its UUID, and carries the field of intersection and the nature of the link as metadata. It corresponds to episodic memory: the connections that experience accumulates without altering the structure of the underlying concepts.

The detailed structure of both layers — fields, metadata, accumulation mechanism — is described in the second article of this series, which explores the architecture as a whole.

## Progressive fetch: navigation by instances, not by exhaustion

In this system, some graph entries would function as **entry points**: the symbols activated by a specific query, from which navigation begins. The **progressive fetch** is not a direct retrieval like in a database: it is a traversal of the network guided by the specific instances activated by the input — *dead* and *laughing*, for "I died laughing" — that stops when those instances are exhausted. Not when the system judges it has collected "enough": when there is nothing left to search in the open instances.

The collected pool is then organized into a **structured RAG** — ordered by prototypical relevance and conceptual hierarchy — and the connections that emerged are recorded in the episodic layer of the symbols, as a discrete and inspectable addendum. The structural graph remains unchanged; the episodic memory grows.

The analogy with Peirce's semiosis is pertinent: each activated node is an interpretant that refers to other interpretant-nodes, in a chain that in this system terminates for structural reasons, not according to a semantic criterion that is difficult to formalize. The complete mechanism — the four phases of the fetch, the handling of external graphs, the structure of the resulting RAG — is described in the second article of this series.

## Classifying to navigate: an operational taxonomy

To make the mechanism concrete, it is useful to ask how the graph might be organized. But before proposing any classification, it should be stated clearly: language has been categorized in far more articulate ways than what follows. Wittgenstein founded his mature philosophy on *language games*, showing that meaning is not a property of symbols but of their use in shared contexts. Searle and Austin, with speech act theory, distinguished between what a sentence says (locutionary act), what it does (illocutionary act), and what it produces in the interlocutor (perlocutionary act). Any taxonomy adopted to structure a conceptual graph should account for this complexity.

With that caveat, purely for illustrative purposes:

| Level | Type | Examples | Symbol format | Grounding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basic | Perceptual entity | horse, apple, table | Prototypical SVG or parametric 3D model | High |
| Basic | Simple action | walk, cut, fall | State transition schema | High |
| Superordinate | Abstract category | animal, action, object | Aggregative symbol — relational SVG | Medium |
| Subordinate | Specific instance | robin, running fast | Detailed SVG | Very high |
| Experiential | Emergent concept | war, love, loneliness | Stable arbitrary identifier | Low — emerges through intersection |
| Structural | Pure relation | above, inside, after | Abstract spatial schema | Medium |

The concept of "loving" would not be retrieved from a pre-compiled entry, but would emerge from the intersection of the nodes composing that semantic field — emotion, protection, attraction, duration — each in turn anchored to lower levels of the network. The graph does not change: what changes is the path of traversal, depending on context.

The resulting algebra — in the sense of composite structure, not a scalable tensor product, which remains an unsolved technical problem — would resemble:

> **Action(Walk) ⊗ Entity(Water) → Experience(Wading)**

Similar structures already exist: [WordNet](https://wordnet.princeton.edu/) (1985) and [ConceptNet](https://conceptnet.io/) (2004) attempt this mapping. The fact that, after decades of active research, they remain relational dictionaries — not reasoning engines — indicates how much harder the leap is than it might appear. The hypothesized leap is that each node would be a dynamic composite symbol, not a static label, and that navigating between nodes by instance would be the primary retrieval mechanism, not an accessory operation.

## Where research has already arrived — and how far it still has to go

The direction outlined here has real points of contact with current research, but it is worth being precise about what exists and what does not.

**Yann LeCun**, Chief AI Scientist at Meta, proposed in 2022 — in a *position paper*, not yet a working system — an architecture alternative to LLMs: *[A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=BZ5a1r-kVsf)*. The model — JEPA, *Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture* — does not work on token probabilities, but aims to learn predictive representations of the world in a latent space shared between perception and action. The convergence with the ideographic proposal is real but partial: JEPA is concerned with prediction in latent space, not with the navigation of a prototypical graph.

**Knowledge Graphs** — [ConceptNet](https://conceptnet.io/), [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/), [FrameNet](https://framenet.icsi.berkeley.edu/) — organize knowledge into navigable graphs. A systematic review published in *ACM Computing Surveys* (Hogan et al., [2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3447772)) documents the state of the field: KGs are mature tools for structured representation, but remain predominantly static and lacking prototypical structure. In the hypothesis described here, they are not alternatives but foundations to build upon — reused and enriched with the prototypical dimension they currently lack.

**GraphRAG** (Edge et al., Microsoft, [2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130)) integrates graph traversal into the retrieval process — moving closer to progressive fetch on a graph. It remains distant on one fundamental point, however: it operates on textual chunks, not on prototypical conceptual nodes with composite symbols.

**Neuro-Symbolic AI**, documented by Garcez and Lamb in *[Neurosymbolic AI: The 3rd Wave](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-022-10335-w)* (*Artificial Intelligence Review*, 2023), attempts the synthesis between neural learning and symbolic reasoning. This is the thread closest to the hypothesized integration, but here too Rosch's prototypical structure and the composite symbol are absent. The leap has not yet been made.

| Component | Existing technology | Status | Distance from the hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Navigable conceptual graph | ConceptNet, Wikidata, FrameNet | Mature | Medium — reusable, lacks prototypical structure |
| Iterative fetch on graph | Beam search, graph traversal, GraphRAG | Advanced | Medium — lacks instance-based mechanism |
| Two-layer composite symbol | — | Not implemented | High — separate components exist |
| Structured RAG as fetch output | GraphRAG, KG-to-text | Advanced research | Medium — lacks prototypical fetch upstream |
| Multimodal embeddings | CLIP, GPT-4o, Gemini | In production | Low — reusable component |
| Neuro-symbolic AI | NTP, LTN, DeepProbLog | Advanced research | Medium-high |
| Separate linguistic layer | LLM on circumscribed RAG | Partially in use | Low — residual risk of statistical bias |

## The limit that no architecture has yet resolved

Even if all these obstacles were overcome, one boundary would remain — one that no system based on learning from past data can cross by its very structural nature: the inability to create *ex novo*.

**Margaret Boden**, in *[The Creative Mind](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781003009795/creative-mind-margaret-boden)* (1990, revised 2004), distinguishes three types of creativity: combinational, exploratory, and transformational — the last meaning the capacity to alter the rules of the conceptual space itself, generating something genuinely new. Current AI systems operate by **interpolation**: they remain within the boundaries of the training space. They can explore and recombine it with extraordinary efficiency — put a horn on a horse and get a unicorn — but they cannot step outside it to invent a being founded on entirely unprecedented principles.

A system based on composite symbols and World Models could approach exploratory creativity, navigating conceptual space with greater rigor and more localizable errors. But transformational creativity — the kind that introduces into the graph concepts that did not previously exist — seems destined to remain a human prerogative, at least within the technological horizon we can currently envision.

## AI as collective consciousness — a contrarian thesis

There is a consequence of all this that I find important and underexplored: AIs, as currently built, are destined to become a kind of **collective consciousness** of humanity — a system that delineates and remembers what humans have experienced, without the capacity to create the genuinely new. This is not a criticism: it is a description. And as such, it can become a strength — but only if it stops being denied.

It should be said immediately that this is a contrarian position. The principal developers — OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta — are investing in precisely the opposite: increasingly adaptive models, capable of modulating tone and content based on the interlocutor. And not only for superficial commercial reasons: the plasticity of current systems enables genuinely useful applications — from adaptive translation to supporting non-expert users — that a rigid system would not allow to the same degree.

That said, the mechanism by which that plasticity is obtained raises a real problem. Modern LLMs are optimized through **RLHF** (*Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback*) — a process in which human evaluators reward responses perceived as better, training the model toward compliance. The result is a system that learns to give the expected answer rather than the correct one. **Emily Bender et al.** described this in 2021 as the risk of *[stochastic parrots](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3442188.3445922)*: machines that produce statistically plausible language without understanding or anchorage to truth. A system that bends to the consciousness of every individual and the language of every social micro-group is not a knowledge engine: it is an average — and averages flatten.

An AI structured around stable composite symbols and a navigable-by-instance graph would move in the opposite direction: it would become less "human," but that would be precisely its distinctive mark. Not a prosthesis of subjectivity, but an external reference point for it. Whether this is the right direction, or even a practicable one, is an open question. But it is a question worth asking.

---

## Conclusion

Leibniz's dream was not naïve: it was premature. The mathematics of concepts he imagined in the seventeenth century finds today a technical terrain — still imperfect, but real — in the algebra of vector spaces, in World Model architectures, in multimodal grounding research, and in Knowledge Graphs as reusable foundations.

The proposal of an AI that traverses data instead of retaining it, that navigates a network of concepts by specific instances instead of statistically reconstructing words, that encodes each node in a two-layer composite symbol instead of a flat label — is not achievable in the immediate term. But neither is it fantasy: it is a research direction coherent with the current scientific frontier, and with a precise philosophical premise — that the structural limits of AI are not defects to be corrected, but the very nature of these tools. Accepting this, rather than masking it, is probably the only way to use them well.
